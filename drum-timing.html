Drum timing

How to improve your drum timing

Good time is the one thing every drummer gets hired for. It is also the thing most of us guess about, because you cannot hear a few milliseconds of rush while you are the one playing. This page covers why timing slips, the drills that fix it, and how to measure it so you stop guessing.

🎯 Timing is two things, not one

When people say a drummer has "good time," they usually mean two separate skills:

  • Accuracy: on average, do your hits land on the beat, early, or late? A drummer who is consistently 15 ms early is accurate in a sense, just early.
  • Consistency: how much do your hits scatter around that average? This is what people feel as "groove" or "pocket." A player can be slightly early all night and still feel great if every hit is early by the same amount.

Most practice advice only talks about the first one. The second one is where the feel lives, and it is the one that fatigue, nerves, and difficult fills chew up first. The math page explains how DrumPulse turns both into a single groove score.

🏃 Why drummers rush (and sometimes drag)

Rushing is the common one. When a part gets louder, busier, or more physical, the body anticipates the next stroke and each hit lands a hair early. Fills rush. Crashes rush. The last chorus rushes because everyone is excited. None of it is a character flaw, it is just how arms and adrenaline work.

Dragging shows up later. Late in a long set, or at the end of a hard drum cardio session, tired muscles respond a little slower and hits land behind the beat. If you track your timing through a whole session you can usually see the exact minute it starts.

Knowing which one you do, and when, is half the fix. The other half is the drills below.

🔢 Drill 1: subdivide out loud

The clock in your head is only as fine as the smallest note you are counting. If you count quarter notes, you have four reference points per bar. If you count sixteenths, you have sixteen, and every hit has a nearby target to land on.

  • Set a metronome to a comfortable tempo, around 70 BPM.
  • Play steady eighth notes and count "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" out loud. Not in your head. Out loud.
  • Switch to sixteenths: "1 e and a 2 e and a." Keep counting while you play.
  • Now play a simple groove while still counting sixteenths. The counting is the exercise, the groove is just the vehicle.

This feels silly for about a week. Then it stops feeling silly and your fills stop rushing, because every note inside the fill now has a name and a place.

🔇 Drill 2: the gap click

A click on every beat does your timekeeping for you. To build your own clock, take the click away and see if you are still there when it comes back.

  • Click on 2 and 4 only. Set the metronome to half your tempo and treat each click as a backbeat. If you rush, the click lands after your snare. If you drag, it lands before.
  • Click on beat 1 only. Metronome at a quarter of the tempo. Now you are on your own for three beats at a time.
  • Click every other bar. Many metronome apps can mute bars. Play one bar with the click, one bar without, and listen for how far off you are when it returns.

Start slow. The goal is not to survive the gap, it is to arrive at the next click without a flinch.

🐢 Drill 3: slow it down, then record it

Playing fast hides timing problems. Playing slow exposes them, because at 60 BPM the space between hits is huge and every wobble is audible. Take the pattern that rushes and play it at a tempo where it feels almost boring. Once it is clean there, move up 5 BPM at a time.

Then record yourself. Your phone's voice memo app is enough. Listening back is the first time most drummers hear what the band hears, and it is humbling in a useful way. Put the recording next to the click and you will hear exactly where you lean.

⌚ Drill 4: measure instead of guessing

⏱️ A 15-minute timing routine

Do this on a practice pad or a kit, with a metronome, most days of the week:

MinutesDrillTempo
3Single strokes, counting sixteenths out loud70 BPM
3Simple groove, click on 2 and 4 only80 BPM (click at 40)
3One fill that usually rushes, slowed down60 BPM, then +5 each clean pass
3Groove with the click muted every other bar90 BPM
3Free play, no click, hold the tempo yourselfWhatever you ended on

If you practice rudiments, run them through the same routine. A paradiddle at 70 BPM with the click on 2 and 4 is a better timing exercise than most grooves.

Timing questions

Why do drummers rush?
Mostly excitement and effort. When a part gets louder, busier, or more physical, the body tends to anticipate the next hit, and each note lands a little early. Fatigue does the opposite late in a set, when hits start landing late. Both are normal and both are fixable with slow, measured practice.
How long does it take to improve drum timing?
Most drummers notice a change within a few weeks of short daily timing work, around 10 to 15 minutes a day with a metronome. Big, lasting changes take months, because timing lives in habit, not knowledge. Measuring your timing each session makes the progress visible so it is easier to keep going.
Should I always practice with a metronome?
No. Use the metronome to build and check your internal clock, then practice without it so you learn to hold tempo on your own. A good split is most technical practice with a click and some free playing or play-along without one. DrumPulse scores both: Metronome mode grades you against the click, Free Play grades you against your own groove.
What is a good drum timing score?
It depends on the tempo and the pattern, so compare yourself with yourself. In DrumPulse the groove score runs 0 to 100 and combines how far your hits land from the beat with how consistent they are. A rising score at the same tempo over a few weeks is the signal that matters, more than any single number.
